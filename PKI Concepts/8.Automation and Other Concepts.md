# Automation and Other Concepts

The final module looks at where PKI is heading. Certificates are getting shorter-lived, there are far more of them, and quantum computing is a long-term threat to today's algorithms. Manual requests and spreadsheets no longer work. This module covers the protocols and tools that automate certificate management (ACME and NDES/SCEP), how a third-party lifecycle management solution works with AD CS, and how post-quantum cryptography is arriving in Microsoft PKI.

The module is split into these topics:

- **ACME** - the standard protocol for fully automated certificate issuance and renewal
- **NDES/SCEP** - enrollment for network devices and MDM-managed devices
- **ADCS + CLM Solution Integration** - adding certificate lifecycle management on top of AD CS
- **PQC** - post-quantum cryptography and what it means for your PKI
- **Module Review**

---

## Why Automation Matters

- **Shorter lifetimes.** The CA/Browser Forum has approved a phased reduction of the maximum lifetime of publicly trusted TLS certificates: 200 days from March 2026, 100 days from March 2027, and 47 days from March 2029. Internal PKIs are not bound by this rule, but the same pressure applies to them, because shorter lifetimes reduce the risk of key compromise.
- **Volume.** At 47 days, a certificate is renewed about eight times a year. For 1,000 certificates this means dozens of renewals every week. Doing this by hand leads to outages.
- **More kinds of systems.** Containers, load balancers, cloud services, IoT and network devices all need certificates, and many of them cannot use Windows autoenrollment.
- **Fewer people who know how.** Automation reduces dependency on a few experts and turns renewal into a monitored process.

## ACME

### What is ACME?

ACME (Automatic Certificate Management Environment) is an open standard, RFC 8555, that lets software request, validate, install and renew certificates automatically, with no human involved. It was created for Let's Encrypt, and is now supported by many public CAs and by private CA products. The 90-day lifetime Let's Encrypt is known for is its own policy choice, not a limit of the protocol. ACME works with any validity period that a CA offers.

### Main parts

- **ACME server** - run by the CA. It exposes a directory of URLs and handles orders and validation.
- **ACME client** - runs on the server or device that needs the certificate. Common clients are Certbot, acme.sh, cert-manager (Kubernetes), Caddy, and Windows-focused clients such as Posh-ACME and win-acme.
- **Account** - the client creates an account with a key pair. Some CAs require external account binding (EAB), where the CA gives the client a key ID and secret to link the ACME account to an existing customer account. This is common for enterprise and private ACME servers.

### How it works

1. The client registers an account with the ACME server.
2. The client creates an order for one or more identifiers (such as DNS names).
3. The server returns challenges for each identifier. The client proves control by completing one.
4. The client submits a certificate signing request (CSR) (called finalising the order).
5. The server issues the certificate, and the client downloads and installs it.
6. A scheduled job runs regularly (often daily) and renews when the certificate nears expiry. A common rule is to renew when about two-thirds of the lifetime has passed.

### Challenge types

| Challenge | How control is proved | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| HTTP-01 | Place a token at a well-known path on port 80 of the domain | Simple. Does not work for wildcards, and needs the server to be reachable from the CA. |
| DNS-01 | Create a specific TXT record in DNS | Works for wildcards and for servers that are not reachable from the internet. Needs DNS API credentials, which must be protected. |
| TLS-ALPN-01 | Answer a special TLS handshake on port 443 | Useful where only port 443 is open. |

Private CAs often replace these with their own validation rules (for example trusting requests from approved networks or pre-approved domains), because internal names cannot be validated from the public internet.

### ACME and AD CS

Active Directory Certificate Services does not support ACME natively. Domain-joined machines use autoenrollment, and devices use NDES/SCEP, but a Linux server, a Kubernetes cluster or a cloud load balancer cannot use either easily. Common ways to close this gap:

| Approach | How it works |
|---|---|
| Third-party ACME front end | A CLM or CA product exposes an ACME endpoint and obtains the certificates from AD CS in the background (see the CLM section). |
| Separate ACME-capable private CA below your root | Run a CA such as Smallstep, EJBCA or a vendor PKI with an ACME server, issued as a subordinate CA from your AD CS root, so devices keep one trust anchor. |
| Cloud-provided ACME endpoint | Services such as AWS Certificate Manager now offer ACME endpoints for public certificates, using 45-day validity. |
| Stay with AD CS and use other methods | Autoenrollment, CEP/CES, NDES/SCEP and scripts for the systems that can use them. |

### Operational points

- **Protect the ACME account key and any DNS credentials.** They can be used to obtain certificates for your domains.
- **Monitor renewals.** With short lifetimes, a silent renewal failure leaves very little time to react. Test a failure on purpose, in a non-production system, to check that alerts and runbooks work.
- **Restrict scope.** Limit which domains each client or account may request, and control wildcard use.
- **Reload the application after renewal**, so it begins to use the new certificate. Use post-renewal hooks.
- Plan for rate limits and outages of the ACME server, and add retries.

### Comparison of enrollment methods

| Method | Best for | Authentication |
|---|---|---|
| Autoenrollment | Domain-joined users and computers | Kerberos / AD |
| NDES/SCEP | Network devices, MDM-managed devices | Challenge password, MDM validation |
| EST (RFC 7030) | Modern devices, IoT | TLS client certificate or credentials |
| ACME | Servers, containers, load balancers, cloud workloads | Challenge proving control of the name |
| CEP/CES | Non-domain and cross-forest Windows clients | Kerberos, password or certificate |

## NDES/SCEP

Module 04 introduced NDES and SCEP briefly. This section goes into how they work and how to operate them.

### What are SCEP and NDES?

- **SCEP** (Simple Certificate Enrollment Protocol) is a protocol (documented in RFC 8894) that lets a device request a certificate over HTTP without a domain account or RPC access to the CA.
- **NDES** (Network Device Enrollment Service) is Microsoft's implementation, a role service of AD CS that runs under IIS. It acts as a registration authority. It receives the SCEP request from the device, checks the challenge, and submits the request to the CA on the device's behalf.

Typical clients are routers, switches, firewalls, load balancers, and mobile devices managed by an MDM such as Microsoft Intune.

### Enrollment flow

1. An administrator or the MDM obtains a one-time challenge password (in classic NDES from the `mscep_admin` page, and with Intune the connector creates it for each request).
2. The device generates a key pair and a CSR, includes the challenge, and sends the request to the NDES URL (for example `http://ndes.contoso.com/certsrv/mscep/mscep.dll`).
3. NDES checks the challenge. With Intune, the NDES Certificate Connector also validates the request with the Intune service, to confirm the request came from an enrolled device.
4. NDES, using its own enrollment agent certificate, submits the request to the Enterprise CA using the template it is configured with.
5. The CA issues the certificate, NDES returns it to the device, and the device installs it.

### Components and requirements

- **Server:** a domain-joined Windows server with the NDES role service and IIS. It is usually separate from the CA.
- **Service account:** a domain account (or other supported managed account) that runs the NDES application pool, with the correct rights on the CA, local IIS groups, and a service principal name (SPN) as needed.
- **Registration authority certificates:** NDES enrolls for two certificates, *Exchange Enrollment Agent (Offline request)* and *CEP Encryption*. The service account needs Enroll permission on them.
- **Certificate templates:** the template that NDES uses to issue to devices must be published on the CA, with Enroll rights for the NDES service account. The template is mapped in the registry under `HKLM\SOFTWARE\Microsoft\Cryptography\MSCEP`, where separate values (for example for signature, encryption and general-purpose requests) point to the template names.
- **Template settings:** commonly the template uses *Supply in the request* for the subject, because the device is not in Active Directory, so tight control of who can request through NDES is vital (Module 04).
- **IIS and TLS:** publish NDES over HTTPS with a valid server certificate where the client supports it. For the Intune connector, HTTPS is required.
- **Intune NDES Certificate Connector:** installed on the NDES server for Intune. It sends validation requests to Intune and, together with the SCEP profile, gives the device the correct URL. For Intune, the IIS request filtering limits (such as maximum URL length and maximum query string) are normally increased so long SCEP requests are not rejected. Follow Microsoft's current Intune NDES guidance for the exact values.
- **Network access:** the NDES URL has to be reachable by the devices, often through a reverse proxy in a perimeter network. The NDES server must reach the CA and domain controllers.

### Challenge passwords

The challenge password is a one-time password that proves the request was authorised. It has a limited lifetime and a limited number can be active at one time (the defaults are set in the registry). In classic NDES, an administrator gets passwords from the NDES admin web page, which needs strict access control. With Intune, the connector generates and validates challenges automatically, which is safer than handing out passwords.

### Security considerations

- SCEP is an older protocol with a shared-secret model, so the strength of the process depends on how the challenge is issued and checked. Do not expose the NDES admin page.
- NDES holds powerful enrollment agent certificates. Treat the server as Tier 0: harden it, restrict who can sign in, and monitor it.
- Use separate templates and, if possible, a separate issuing CA for NDES-issued certificates. Limit the EKUs to what devices need (for example Client Authentication for Wi-Fi).
- Do not let the NDES service account have more rights than it needs, and do not use it for other purposes.
- Use HTTPS, and place NDES behind a reverse proxy or application gateway that only allows the SCEP paths.
- Monitor issuance through NDES in the CA logs and look for unusual volume.

### Troubleshooting NDES

- Browse to the `mscep.dll` URL on the NDES server. A healthy service shows a page about the Network Device Enrollment Service. An HTTP 500 or similar error usually points to permissions, IIS or registry template mapping problems.
- Check the Application log on the NDES server and the CA's event log for the matching request.
- Template mismatch or missing permissions are common causes, as is the NDES account not having Enroll permission on the template and the RA certificates.
- Clock differences, an unreachable CA, or a certificate that has expired (such as the RA certificate or the NDES web server certificate) also break enrollment.
- For Intune, check the connector status in the Intune admin center, the connector logs, and that the device can reach the NDES URL.

### NDES versus alternatives

Microsoft Cloud PKI (Module 07) provides a hosted SCEP service for Intune-managed devices, which can replace NDES where all you need is device certificates. NDES is still useful where certificates must come from AD CS and where network devices need SCEP.

## ADCS + CLM Solution Integration

### What is CLM?

In this topic, CLM means **Certificate Lifecycle Management** products from vendors (not the Windows lifecycle notifications mentioned in Module 05). A CLM solution gives one place to discover, issue, deploy, renew, monitor and revoke certificates from many CAs, including AD CS. Examples of vendors in this area are Keyfactor, Venafi (CyberArk), AppViewX, DigiCert Trust Lifecycle Manager, Sectigo, Evertrust and Encryption Consulting. These are examples only.

### Why add CLM to AD CS

AD CS issues certificates well, but it has limits. It does not give an inventory of certificates outside the CA, has no native ACME or EST support, and offers little help with deployment and renewal for non-Windows systems. A CLM solution fills those gaps.

| Capability | What it adds |
|---|---|
| Discovery | Scans networks, servers, load balancers, certificate stores and CA databases to find all certificates, including unknown and expired ones. |
| Inventory and ownership | A single list of certificates with owners, applications, expiry and key details. |
| Policy and governance | Rules for allowed key sizes, algorithms, validity and CAs, with approval workflows. |
| Automated issuance and renewal | Requests and installs certificates through agents, connectors or APIs, and renews them before expiry. |
| Protocol support | Front ends such as ACME, EST and SCEP over the existing AD CS back end. |
| Alerts and reporting | Expiry warnings, compliance and audit reports. |
| Revocation and response | Bulk revoke and replace if a CA or algorithm must be retired. |
| Integrations | Web servers, load balancers, Kubernetes, cloud services, secrets managers, DevOps pipelines and ticketing. |
| Crypto-agility | Helps find where weak algorithms are used, which is the first step in a PQC migration. |

### Common integration architectures

1. **CA connector or gateway.** The CLM platform connects to AD CS (through a gateway or connector service on a Windows server, using the CA's standard interfaces) and requests certificates using templates. The connector uses a service account with Enroll permission on selected templates and Request permission on the CA. It can also synchronise the CA database so that certificates issued directly by AD CS are visible in the CLM inventory.
2. **Agents or orchestrators on the endpoints.** Software on servers or devices generates the key, requests a certificate through the CLM, installs it, binds it to the application and restarts it when needed.
3. **Protocol front end.** The CLM acts as an ACME or EST server for clients, and uses AD CS as the CA behind it.
4. **Policy or exit module on the CA.** Some products add a module on the CA to enforce policy or to send events to the CLM (Module 06). Test and track any custom module carefully, as it runs inside the CA.

### Typical implementation steps

1. **Discover first.** Find out what certificates exist, who owns them and where they are used.
2. **Define policy.** Decide approved algorithms, key sizes, validity periods, naming and which CAs and templates are allowed.
3. **Prepare AD CS.** Create dedicated templates for CLM-driven issuance, with the right EKUs and permissions. Publish them on the chosen issuing CA.
4. **Create the integration account.** Use a dedicated service account with the minimum rights. Decide whether the template needs *Supply in the request* (common for server certificates) and protect it with approval and scope limits.
5. **Connect the CA to the CLM platform** and test issuance, renewal and revocation.
6. **Pilot** with one application team, then expand in waves.
7. **Automate and monitor.** Enable automatic renewal, alerts and dashboards. Include the CLM in your backup and disaster recovery plans (Module 06).
8. **Review regularly.** Check owners, orphaned certificates and policy exceptions.

### Security and operational points

- The CLM integration account can request certificates on your behalf, so it is a high-value target. Protect it like a Tier 0 account, with monitoring, restricted logon and credential protection.
- Limit which templates and subject names the account can request, and keep *Supply in the request* templates tightly scoped (the ESC1 lessons from Module 04).
- Keep an audit trail of every issuance and who or what requested it, and send it to your SIEM.
- Plan high availability for the CLM and its connectors. If it fails, renewals stop, so monitor its health.
- Keep AD CS knowledge in the team. A CLM does not remove the need for CA maintenance, CRL management and monitoring (Modules 02 and 06).
- Check that the vendor supports your AD CS version and any PQC algorithms that you plan to use.

## PQC

### The quantum threat

Post-quantum cryptography (PQC) means algorithms that are believed to be secure against both classical and quantum computers. A large, fault-tolerant quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could break RSA, Diffie-Hellman and elliptic-curve cryptography, which are the basis of almost all certificates and key exchange today. Grover's algorithm weakens symmetric keys and hashes only by a factor of two in effect, so AES-256 and SHA-256 or SHA-384 remain acceptable.

Two different risks drive the timeline:

- **Harvest now, decrypt later.** An attacker records encrypted traffic today and decrypts it when a quantum computer exists. This affects key exchange and encryption, and is the most urgent concern for data that must stay secret for many years.
- **Forged signatures.** Once quantum computers exist, an attacker could forge signatures, including certificates and code signing. Signature algorithms must be replaced before that day, and long-lived keys such as root CAs need to be planned for early.

### NIST standards

| Standard | Algorithm | Origin | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| FIPS 203 | ML-KEM | CRYSTALS-Kyber | Key encapsulation (key exchange) |
| FIPS 204 | ML-DSA | CRYSTALS-Dilithium | Digital signatures |
| FIPS 205 | SLH-DSA | SPHINCS+ | Digital signatures (hash-based, conservative) |
| FN-DSA | FALCON | | Digital signatures (smaller signatures, in development) |

NIST published the first three in 2024 and has also selected further algorithms for standardisation. NIST's guidance also proposes retiring RSA and elliptic-curve algorithms over the coming decade, so check the latest NIST timeline. Government rules, such as the NSA's CNSA 2.0, set earlier deadlines for national security systems.

### What changes for a PKI

- **Bigger keys and signatures.** For example, an ML-DSA-65 public key is about 2 KB and a signature about 3.3 KB, compared with 256 bytes for an RSA-2048 signature. Certificates, certificate chains, CRLs and TLS handshakes all become larger. This can affect devices with limited memory or bandwidth, and software with fixed buffer sizes.
- **New algorithm identifiers.** Software must understand the new OIDs for ML-DSA and ML-KEM.
- **HSM and key storage support.** HSMs and key storage providers need firmware and drivers for PQC. Older HSMs may need replacement.
- **Hybrid and composite approaches.** During the transition, many designs combine a classical and a post-quantum algorithm, so security holds if either one is broken. IETF is defining composite certificate formats, but they are not yet universally available.
- **Trust stores and applications.** Public browser trust for PQC certificates is still developing. For now, PQC certificates are mainly practical in private PKIs, where you control both ends.

### Microsoft and AD CS

- **Platform APIs.** ML-KEM and ML-DSA reached general availability in the Windows cryptography (CNG) APIs on Windows Server 2025 and Windows 11 24H2/25H2 in late 2025.
- **AD CS ML-DSA support.** The May 2026 security update (KB5087539) for Windows Server 2025 brought ML-DSA to AD CS. A CA can use ML-DSA-44, ML-DSA-65 and ML-DSA-87, which are selected from the algorithm list when creating the CA key or certificate templates. This allows issuing and root CAs to sign with quantum-resistant algorithms.
- **Still planned.** ML-KEM and composite algorithms in AD CS are planned for a later phase, so today AD CS support is for signatures, not key exchange certificates.
- **Older servers.** The support applies to Windows Server 2025 with the update or later. No backport is expected to Windows Server 2019 or 2022, so a PQC pilot generally means building new CAs on Windows Server 2025.
- **Early stage.** Use it first for evaluation, testing and non-critical uses such as code signing in a lab, and watch client and application support.

### Getting ready: a practical plan

1. **Crypto inventory.** Find every place that uses RSA, ECC, certificates and key exchange: CAs, templates, TLS endpoints, VPN, code signing, S/MIME, devices and applications. A CLM tool can help (see the previous topic).
2. **Prioritise.** Start with long-lived keys (such as root and issuing CAs and code-signing keys) and long-term confidential data.
3. **Build crypto-agility.** Make algorithm choices configuration items, not hard-coded values. Avoid designs that cannot be changed without rebuilding the PKI.
4. **Check dependencies.** Ask HSM, CLM, device and application vendors about their PQC support and timelines.
5. **Pilot a parallel hierarchy.** On Windows Server 2025 with the update, test a separate two-tier PQC hierarchy (for example ML-DSA-87 for the root and ML-DSA-65 for the issuing CA). Check certificate size, chain building, revocation, applications and devices.
6. **Plan hybrid deployment where possible**, and plan how clients will trust both hierarchies during the transition.
7. **Plan for size.** Look at CRL size, delta CRLs, OCSP responses, and web server and device limits.
8. **Update policy and documentation.** Add PQC to your certificate policy, standards and runbooks, and track the standards as they evolve.

## Module Review

- Shorter certificate lifetimes (200 days from March 2026, 100 days in March 2027, 47 days in March 2029 for public TLS) and growing volume make automation essential.
- **ACME** (RFC 8555) automates issuance and renewal through account, order, challenge, finalise and download steps. Challenges are HTTP-01, DNS-01 (needed for wildcards) and TLS-ALPN-01. AD CS has no native ACME support, so use a third-party front end, a separate ACME-capable CA under your root, or other methods.
- **NDES** is Microsoft's SCEP implementation, running in IIS and acting as a registration authority. It uses enrollment agent certificates, template mappings in the registry and one-time challenge passwords. It is used for network devices and Intune-managed devices through the Intune NDES Certificate Connector, and must be secured as Tier 0. Microsoft Cloud PKI can replace it for device-only needs.
- **CLM products** add discovery, inventory, policy, automated renewal, protocol front ends (ACME, EST, SCEP), reporting and integrations on top of AD CS. Integrate through a connector with a least-privilege service account, dedicated templates and full auditing.
- **PQC** is about quantum-resistant algorithms: ML-KEM (FIPS 203) for key exchange, ML-DSA (FIPS 204) and SLH-DSA (FIPS 205) for signatures. Harvest-now-decrypt-later makes key exchange urgent, and long-lived CA keys need early planning.
- AD CS supports ML-DSA on Windows Server 2025 since the May 2026 update (KB5087539). ML-KEM and composite algorithms are planned. Windows Server 2022 and earlier are not expected to receive PQC support in AD CS.
- Start now with a crypto inventory, crypto-agility, vendor checks and a pilot parallel hierarchy, and expect larger keys, signatures and certificates.

## Common Interview Questions

**What is ACME and why does it matter?**
An IETF standard (RFC 8555) for automated certificate issuance and renewal, essential as certificate lifetimes shrink toward 47 days.

**Which ACME challenge works for wildcard certificates?**
DNS-01, because the client proves control by creating a DNS TXT record.

**Does AD CS support ACME?**
Not natively. Use a third-party ACME front end or CLM, an ACME-capable CA under your AD CS root, or other enrollment methods.

**What is the difference between SCEP and NDES?**
SCEP is the protocol. NDES is Microsoft's server implementation of it, working as a registration authority in front of an Enterprise CA.

**How does NDES get certificates from the CA?**
Using its own enrollment agent certificates (Exchange Enrollment Agent (Offline request) and CEP Encryption), it submits the request on the device's behalf, using the template mapped in the registry.

**How does Intune use NDES?**
The device sends a SCEP request to NDES. The Intune NDES Certificate Connector validates it with the Intune service, and then NDES requests the certificate from the CA.

**What are the main risks of NDES?**
A shared-challenge model, powerful enrollment agent certificates, and broad templates such as Supply in the request. Mitigate with HTTPS, a restricted URL path, dedicated templates and Tier 0 hardening.

**What is CLM and how does it integrate with AD CS?**
Certificate Lifecycle Management software that discovers and manages certificates. It connects to AD CS through a connector with a least-privilege service account and dedicated templates, and adds automation, ACME/EST, reporting and integrations.

**Why is PQC urgent even before quantum computers exist?**
Harvest now, decrypt later threatens long-lived confidential data, and CA keys with long lifetimes need to be replaced early.

**Which PQC algorithms does AD CS support, and on which OS?**
ML-DSA (44, 65, 87) on Windows Server 2025 with the May 2026 update (KB5087539) or later. ML-KEM and composite algorithms are planned.

**What are the practical impacts of PQC on a PKI?**
Larger keys, signatures, certificates and CRLs, new OIDs, HSM and software support requirements, and a need for hybrid transition planning.
